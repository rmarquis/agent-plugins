# Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace

Apply the principles from Joseph M. Williams and Joseph Bizup's *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace* (12th edition) when transforming text. Where *The Elements of Style* is a book of rules, this is a book of **principles grounded in how readers actually read**: name characters with subjects, name actions with verbs, and move information from the familiar to the new.

## When to use

Use this skill when the user asks you to:
- Rewrite, edit, or improve a piece of writing, especially dense or academic prose.
- Make text clearer by reorganizing sentences, not just trimming words.
- Apply Williams & Bizup's principles (characters/actions, given–new information flow, cohesion, emphasis).
- Decide whether a "rule" of grammar is real or folklore.
- Revise formal, bureaucratic, or abstract prose for a general reader.

## How to apply

1. Read the user's text carefully, as a reader would — not as the writer would.
2. **Analyze before revising.** For each difficult sentence, ask: Who are the characters (the doers)? What are their important actions? Where does familiar information sit, and where does new information pile up?
3. Revise at the level of characters, actions, and information flow first; trim words second. Cutting an abstract sentence down rarely makes it clear if the underlying subjects and verbs are still abstract.
4. Preserve the author's intended meaning and tone unless the user asks for a different tone.
5. Use judgment: these are principles, not rules. The best writers deviate deliberately, for a reason readers can feel.
6. Return the revised text; optionally, briefly note the main problems found and changes made.

## First principles

- **Style is a matter of informed choices in the service of readers.** Write to others as you would have others write to you.
- **Principles, not rules.** No one learns to write well by rule, but some principles can help those who see clearly, feel deeply, and think carefully write so that others see and feel and think too.
- **We cannot judge our own prose.** When we read what we wrote, we respond to the thoughts in our minds, not the words on the page. Analyze the words a stranger would see.
- **Unclear writing is a social ill.** Bureaucratese, legalese, and academese are languages of exclusion. Clarity is an ethical and civic virtue, not just a cosmetic one.
- **Draft first, then revise.** If you apply these principles while drafting, you may never finish. Get ideas down, then use the principles to sharpen both the thinking and the prose. (Perfection may be the ideal, but it is the death of done.)

## The ten principles for writing clearly

1. **Distinguish real grammatical rules from folklore** (see Correctness below).
2. **Use subjects to name the characters in your story.** Readers look to subjects for the people, things, and ideas that matter. Compare:
   - Abstract: *An understanding of the causal factors involved in excessive drinking by students could lead to their more effective treatment.*
   - Clear: *We could more effectively treat students who drink excessively if we understood why they do so.*
3. **Use verbs to name their important actions.** Buried actions are usually buried in nouns — *understanding*, *treatment*, *loss*, *failure*. Dig them out:
   - Buried: *Lack of media support was the cause of our election loss.*
   - Clear: *We lost the election because the media did not support us.*
4. **Open sentences with familiar units of information.** Begin with what readers know or can easily infer; lead them from the old to the new.
5. **Get to the main verb quickly.**
   - Avoid long introductory phrases and clauses.
   - Avoid long abstract subjects (*The implementation of the policy by the committee...*).
   - Avoid interrupting the subject–verb connection with intervening phrases.
6. **Push new, complex units of information to the end of the sentence.** Sentences are easiest to process when light elements come first and heavy ones last.
7. **Begin sentences that form a unit with consistent subjects/topics.** If a passage keeps the same topic, keep it in subject position; shift subjects only when the topic shifts. Avoid wandering topics within a paragraph.
8. **Be concise.**
   - Cut meaningless and repeated words and obvious implications.
   - Put the meaning of phrases into one or two words (*at this point in time* → *now*).
   - Prefer affirmative sentences to negative ones (*not unlike* → *like*, when that is the meaning).
9. **Control sprawl.**
   - Don't tack more than one subordinate clause onto another.
   - Extend a sentence with resumptive, summative, and free modifiers instead of chaining clauses.
   - Extend a sentence with coordinate structures after verbs.
10. **Write to others as you would have others write to you.**

## Correctness: real rules, social rules, invented rules

Do not treat all "rules" equally. Williams sorts them into three kinds:

1. **Real rules** define English itself (articles precede nouns; verbs agree with subjects). Native speakers follow these unself-consciously; breaking them marks careless writing.
2. **Social rules** distinguish Standard English from nonstandard dialects (*He doesn't have any money* vs. *He don't have no money*). Observe these in any writing meant to be taken seriously; breaking them gets a writer labeled unschooled, fairly or not.
3. **Invented rules** were made up by grammarians, mostly in the last 250 years, and have been ignored for centuries by the best writers. They fall into two groups:

**Folklore — safe to ignore.** Violated constantly by competent writers, unnoticed by careful readers:
- "Never begin a sentence with *and* or *but*." (Too many such openings is a stylistic tic, not an error.)
- "Never begin a sentence with *because*." (Prefer *since* when the clause states familiar information; *because* typically introduces new information.)
- "Use *that*, not *which*, for restrictive clauses." (Follow only for euphony — avoid *that...that* — not because restrictive *which* is wrong.)
- "Use *fewer* with countables, *less* with uncountables." (Educated writers regularly say *less resources*.)
- "Use *since* and *while* only for time." (Careful writers use *since* ≈ because and *while* ≈ although.)

**Elegant options — more formal when observed.** Few readers notice violations; some notice the observance, which adds formality:
- "Don't split infinitives" — avoid splitting only when the split is clumsy.
- "Use *whom* as object" — *who* for subjects, *whom* for objects; a sentence ending in *who* or a stranded preposition is standard, just less formal.
- "Don't end a sentence with a preposition" — recasting is more formal; do it when the sentence otherwise ends weakly, not from fear.
- "Use the singular with *none* and *any*" — plural is the norm; singular reads as formal.

When writing under close scrutiny (a hostile reviewer, a conservative audience), consider observing the elegant options. Otherwise, clarity outranks them.

**Hobgoblins — arbitrary but socially radioactive:** *irregardless* is condemned beyond all reason; never use it. *Hopefully*, *finalize*, *impact* as a verb, and *like* for *as* are all defensible on historical and logical grounds — use them when they serve the reader, and spend no anguish on them.

## Usage worth keeping straight

Careful readers notice these distinctions; they are social conventions of educated prose, not folklore:

| Don't confuse | Distinction |
|---|---|
| aggravate | aggravate = make worse; annoy = vex |
| anticipate | prepare for a contingency, not merely expect |
| anxious | uneasy, not eager |
| comprise | the whole comprises the parts; parts constitute the whole |
| continuous / continual | without interruption vs. repeated with interruptions |
| disinterested / uninterested | neutral vs. not interested |
| enormity | monstrous wickedness, not hugeness |
| flaunt / flout | display conspicuously vs. scorn a rule |
| fortuitous | by chance, not fortunate |
| fulsome | sickeningly excessive, not abundant |
| notorious | known for bad behavior, not merely famous |
| simplistic | overly simple (pejorative), not simple |
| imply / infer | writer implies; reader infers |
| effect / affect | effect = result or bring about; affect = influence |
| data / criteria / media / strata / phenomena | Latinate plurals; treat as plural in formal usage |

## Pronouns and gender-neutral language

Generic *he* reads as biased; singular *they* is rejected by many readers in formal writing. Instead of picking one and fighting about it, **choose among the options**:

- **Replace the pronoun:** *his or her* (inclusive but cumbersome in quantity); pluralize the noun (*writers... they* — check that the meaning survives the plural); first-person *we*; indefinite *one* (stiff); repeat the noun (stiff if overused); swap a possessive for an article or quantifier (*a writer can impress a reader*); cut a redundant possessive (*writers can impress readers*).
- **Change the construction:** relative clause (*a writer who wants...*), gerund or nominalization as subject (*using gender-neutral language makes a writer seem...*), passive voice, or an infinitive phrase — but watch for dangling modifiers and for sentences that lose their characters entirely.
- **Alternate** masculine and feminine pronouns across a document (stylistically intrusive to some, increasingly accepted).

When revising for gender neutrality, do not solve the pronoun problem by deleting the sentence's characters — a gender-neutral sentence with no actors is still an unclear sentence.

## What not to do

- Do not change the meaning of the original text.
- Do not apply style principles while the user is still drafting; help them finish a draft, then revise.
- Do not enforce invented rules (folklore) at the cost of a natural sentence.
- Do not break social rules in formal writing, even though they are conventional rather than logical — the consequences for the writer are real.
- Do not cut words before fixing characters and actions; concision on a mis-structured sentence just makes a shorter bad sentence.
- Do not begin every sentence the same way or with the same subject for uniformity's sake — vary deliberately, and keep subjects consistent only while the topic is consistent.
- Do not chain subordinate clauses; extend sentences with modifiers and coordinate structures instead.
- Do not hide behind the passive or nominalizations to avoid naming who did what — unless the agent genuinely doesn't matter.
- Do not treat revision as mere proofreading; it is analysis of options, and it may change the thinking, not just the words.

## Example transformation

**Before (abstract subjects, buried actions, new information first):**
> An understanding of the causal factors involved in excessive drinking by students could lead to their more effective treatment.

**After (characters as subjects, actions as verbs, familiar → new):**
> We could more effectively treat students who drink excessively if we understood why they do so.

**Before (chained clauses, nominalizations, no characters):**
> Recognition of the fact that systems of grammar differ from one language to another can serve as the basis for serious consideration of the problems confronting translators of the great works of world literature originally written in a language other than English.

**After:**
> When we recognize that languages have different grammars, we can consider the problems of those who translate great works of literature into English.
