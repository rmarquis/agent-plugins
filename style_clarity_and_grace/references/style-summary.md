# Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace — Quick Reference

A compact summary of Joseph M. Williams and Joseph Bizup, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*, 12th edition (Pearson, 2017), based on the excerpt PDF in the parent directory (front matter, the Ten Principles, and Lessons 1–2). For the full explanations and examples, see the source PDF or the complete book.

## The book's argument in one paragraph

Good style is not obedience to rules but a series of **informed choices in the service of readers**. None of us can judge our own writing as others will, because when we read our own prose we respond to the thoughts in our minds, not the words on the page. The cure is to learn **principles, not rules**: once you understand why readers judge one sentence dense and abstract and another clear and direct, you can analyze your own prose and revise it. Unclear writing — bureaucratese, legalese, academese — is a social ill and, in its extreme forms, a language of exclusion a democracy cannot tolerate.

## Ten principles for writing clearly

1. Distinguish real grammatical rules from folklore.
2. Use **subjects** to name the characters in your story.
3. Use **verbs** to name their important actions.
4. Open sentences with **familiar units of information**.
5. Get to the main verb quickly:
   - avoid long introductory phrases and clauses;
   - avoid long abstract subjects;
   - avoid interrupting the subject–verb connection.
6. Push new, complex units of information to the **end** of the sentence.
7. Begin sentences that form a unit with **consistent subjects/topics**.
8. Be concise:
   - cut meaningless and repeated words and obvious implications;
   - put the meaning of phrases into one or two words;
   - prefer affirmative sentences to negative ones.
9. Control sprawl:
   - don't tack more than one subordinate clause onto another;
   - extend a sentence with resumptive, summative, and free modifiers;
   - extend a sentence with coordinate structures after verbs.
10. Above all, **write to others as you would have others write to you**.

## Lesson 1: Understanding Style — key points

- The book rests on two beliefs: it is good to write clearly, and anyone can.
- Principles of sentence clarity apply to prose of all kinds; advice on motivation, global organization, and sources targets academic and professional writing.
- Unclear writing has a 450-year history in English; bad models (Mulcaster, academic prose, even Orwell criticizing turgid prose in turgid prose) teach readers to imitate opacity.
- Private causes of unclear writing: inflating prose to seem deep, hiding ignorance behind abstraction, freezing up in new fields, and — the biggest — not knowing when readers will find us unclear because our own writing always seems clear to us.
- **Drafting vs. revising:** if you think about these principles while drafting, you may never finish. Get ideas down first; then use the principles to refine your thinking and fix sentences likely to give readers problems. Perfection is the ideal, but it is the death of done.
- "No one learns to write well by rule... but some principles can."

## Lesson 2: Correctness — key points

### Three kinds of rules

1. **Real rules** — define what makes English English (articles precede nouns: *the book*, not *book the*). Native speakers follow these unself-consciously.
2. **Social rules** — distinguish Standard English from nonstandard (*He doesn't have any money* vs. *He don't have no money*). Schooled writers observe these naturally; violating them gets a writer labeled unschooled.
3. **Invented rules** — made up by grammarians, mostly since the mid-18th century, and ignored for 300 years by the best writers. Only these vex writers who already control Standard English.

### Invented rules: folklore (safe to ignore)

- "Don't begin sentences with *and* or *but*." (Beginning *too many* sentences with *and* is a stylistic error, not a grammar error.)
- "Don't begin a sentence with *because*." (Use *since* for a clause giving familiar information; *because* introduces new information.)
- "Use *that*, not *which*, for restrictive clauses." (Fowler 1906; even Fowler admitted most best writers don't comply. Follow it for the sound, not because restrictive *which* is wrong.)
- "Use *fewer* with count nouns, *less* with mass nouns." (No one says *fewer dirt*; educated writers say *less resources*.)
- "Use *since* and *while* only for time." (Careful writers regularly use *since* ≈ because and *while* ≈ although.)

If competent writers regularly violate an alleged rule and careful readers never notice, the rule has no force.

### Invented rules: elegant options (more formal when observed)

- "Don't split infinitives" — avoiding splits can make you sound self-consciously correct.
- "Use *whom* as the object of a verb or preposition" — real rule: *who* as subject in its own clause, *whom* as object; but terminal *who* is standard.
- "Don't end a sentence with a preposition" — correct either way; the shifted version is more formal (and can end a sentence weakly).
- "Use the singular with *none* and *any*" — most writers treat them as plural; singular sounds more formal.

Observing all of these all the time earns little credit but marks your prose as formal. Under close scrutiny, consider observing them.

### Hobgoblins (zealously enforced, no basis)

- *like* for *as* (accepted since the 18th century; Gowers moved it to "Sturdy Indefensibles")
- *hopefully* to mean "I hope" (parallel to *candidly*, *frankly*, *sadly*; dates from mid-20th century)
- *finalize* — means "clean up the last few details," a sense no other word captures
- *impact* as a verb (a verb for 400 years)
- Modifying absolutes (*more perfect*, *very unique*) — generally worth following anyway
- ***Never* use *irregardless*** — arbitrary, but follow it: some will judge you irredeemable

### Words that attract special attention

| Word | Means | Does not mean |
|------|-------|---------------|
| aggravate | make worse | annoy |
| anticipate | prepare for a contingency | expect |
| anxious | uneasy | eager |
| blackmail | extort by threatening to reveal information | coerce |
| cohort | a group attending on someone | a single companion |
| comprise | include all parts in one unit | compose / constitute |
| continuous | without interruption | continual (repeated with interruptions) |
| disinterested | neutral | uninterested |
| enormity | hugely bad | enormous |
| flaunt | display conspicuously | flout (scorn a rule) |
| fortuitous | by chance | fortunate |
| fulsome | sickeningly excessive | much |
| notorious | known for bad behavior | famous |
| simplistic | overly simple (pejorative) | simple |

Also keep straight: imply/infer, principal/principle, accept/except, capital/capitol, affect/effect, proceed/precede, discrete/discreet. Latinate plurals: datum/data, criterion/criteria, medium/media, stratum/strata, phenomenon/phenomena.

You can't predict usage by logic or general rule; learn the rules one by one and accept that many are arbitrary.

### Pronouns and gender-neutral language

- Pronouns should agree with their antecedents, but singular common nouns (*teacher*, *student*) and indefinite pronouns (*someone*, *everyone*) force a choice.
- Generic *he* reads as biased; singular *they* is considered improper by many in formal writing. **The thing to remember is that we have choices:**
  1. Replace the gendered pronoun: *his or her*; pluralize (*writers... they*); first-person *we*; indefinite *one*; repeat the noun; swap a possessive for an article (*a reader*, *each reader*); cut a redundant possessive.
  2. Choose a different construction: relative clause (*a writer who...*), gerund or nominalization (*using gender-neutral language makes...*), passive voice, infinitive phrase — but watch for dangling modifiers and lost characters.
  3. Alternate masculine and feminine pronouns (stylistically intrusive to some, but increasingly popular).

## Drafting and revising

- Draft for ideas; revise for readers. Writing and revising feed each other: clearer expression produces better understanding, which produces clearer expression.
- The 12th edition frames revision as **analysis of options**, not diagnosis and repair of faults.
- Apply the book's principles after drafting, not during — otherwise you may never finish.
